Extreme heat · College Station, Texas

When is it too hot to keep working, and what does the heat do that you can't see?

A construction company in College Station has to decide when crews work, how often they rest, and how long a break needs to be. This page models a typical July day and shows how the time of day, the kind of work, and the break schedule combine to decide whether workers stay within federal heat-safety limits.

The issue in plain language

Your body makes heat whenever you work, and the harder you work, the more heat it makes. On a cool day that heat escapes easily. On a College Station summer afternoon, hot and humid air makes it hard for sweat to cool you, and the sun adds even more heat. When the body cannot get rid of heat fast enough, core temperature climbs.

Most companies watch for the problems they can see: heat stroke, fainting (heat syncope), and severe dehydration. Those are real and can kill. But research also links repeated overheating to damage that builds up quietly over a summer and over a career: kidney injury, strain on the heart, and, after a heat stroke, lasting brain problems. A schedule that avoids collapses can still let workers spend hours each week above the safe limit.

The safe limit used here comes from NIOSH, the federal research agency for worker safety. Its limits say how much heat a healthy worker can handle at a given workload; they are meant to keep core body temperature within about 1 °C (1.8 °F) of normal. When conditions are over the limit, the fix is to rest part of each hour so the hour's average stays under it. [5, 6]

Why this matters in Texas: construction is about 7% of the U.S. workforce but more than a third of heat-related worker deaths, and Texas had more construction heat deaths from 2011 to 2023 than any other state. [11] There is no final federal heat standard, and a 2023 Texas law blocked cities from requiring water breaks, so break schedules are largely up to employers. [9, 10]

What to notice when you move the sliders

  • Heat and workload multiply each other. At 7 a.m., heavy work needs no extra rest. By mid-afternoon the same work needs a large share of each hour off. Switch the workload to light and the afternoon problem mostly disappears. A hotter day hurts a heavy-work crew far more than a light-work crew, so the high-temperature slider matters most when the workload slider is high.
  • The hottest time is mid-to-late afternoon, not noon. Air temperature peaks around 4 p.m., and the sun is still strong then. Heat stress at work peaks between about 2 and 4 p.m.
  • Moving the start time is the cheapest fix. Slide the start time earlier and watch the red minutes shrink. The same 10 hours of work produce much less over-limit time when they avoid the afternoon.
  • A fixed break rule is not enough on its own. About 8 minutes of rest per hour can be plenty in the morning and far too little in the afternoon. Breaks should get longer as the day heats up.
  • New workers are at higher risk. Uncheck "acclimatized." NIOSH sets a lower limit for workers who have not had about a week or two to adjust to heat, and red minutes appear sooner. [6]
  • Shade matters. Uncheck "direct sun" to see how much shade over the work area lowers heat stress.

Why "no one collapsed" is not the same as "no one was harmed"

The red minutes above are the exposure that research links to damage that does not show up the same day. There is no validated formula that turns minutes over the limit into a percentage chance of kidney or heart disease, so this page does not invent one. It counts the exposure; the research below shows why that exposure is worth reducing.

Kidneys

Sweating and a rising core temperature reduce blood flow to the kidneys. In one summer-long study of construction workers in Saudi Arabia, 18% developed signs of kidney injury. [12] A 2025 U.S. study of construction workers found that higher core temperature during a shift predicted markers of acute kidney injury by the end of that shift. [13] Researchers suspect that repeated injuries like this can add up to chronic kidney disease. [13, 15]

Heart

To cool down, the heart pumps more blood to the skin while dehydration shrinks the blood volume it has to work with. Heart rate climbs and the heart works harder for the whole time a worker is overheated. Researchers say the link between heat and heart disease in the general population is well established, but long-term studies of workers are still scarce. [16] Workers with high blood pressure, diabetes, or heart disease are more vulnerable. [17]

Brain

Heat stroke happens when core temperature passes about 40 °C (104 °F) and the brain starts to malfunction. In a review of published heat stroke cases, about a third of survivors whose outcome was known had permanent nerve or brain damage, often to the cerebellum, which controls balance and coordination. Many were young and healthy. [18, 19] Every hour over the limit raises the chance of reaching that point.

How the model works

Show the method and its assumptions
  1. Temperature through the day. The low is fixed at 75 °F, the 1991–2020 normal July low at Easterwood Field. [1] Temperature rises along a smooth curve from the low at 6 a.m. to the slider's high at 4 p.m., then falls back overnight. The timing is a standard modeling assumption, not a measured average.
  2. Humidity. The dew point is held at 72 °F, the July average at Easterwood Field. [2] Relative humidity is computed from temperature and dew point, so it is highest in the morning and lowest in the afternoon, as in real summer days.
  3. WBGT in the shade = 0.7 × wet-bulb temperature + 0.3 × air temperature, the formula for no direct sun. [5] Wet-bulb temperature is computed from air temperature and humidity with Stull's equation. [4]
  4. WBGT in the sun = 0.7 × wet bulb + 0.2 × globe temperature + 0.1 × air temperature. [5] Weather stations do not measure globe temperature, so this page assumes it runs up to 12 °C (22 °F) above air temperature when the sun is highest, scaled by the sun's angle over College Station in mid-July. This is an assumption chosen to sit within published examples, where sunlit globe temperatures are roughly 5 to 17 °C above air temperature. [8] It ignores wind and clouds, so a breezy or overcast day will be less severe.
  5. Safe limit. NIOSH's limit for acclimatized workers is 56.7 − 11.5 × log10(metabolic rate in watts), in °C WBGT; for unacclimatized workers it is 59.9 − 14.1 × log10(watts). [6] Metabolic rates are OSHA's typical values for a 70 kg (154 lb) worker: rest 115 W, light 180 W, moderate 300 W, heavy 415 W, very heavy 520 W. [7]
  6. Rest needed. Following NIOSH's guidance to compare hourly time-weighted averages, the page finds the largest share of each hour that can be spent working (with the rest spent sitting in the shade) while the hour's average WBGT stays at or under the limit for the hour's average metabolic rate. [6]
  7. Over-limit minutes = rest needed − rest given, when that is positive. The summer total multiplies one day by 65 workdays (13 weeks of June–August, 5 days a week).

Limits of this model. It describes a typical day, not a forecast; real worksites should use an on-site WBGT meter. [7] NIOSH limits are designed for healthy workers. Age, obesity, some medications, and conditions such as diabetes or heart disease lower heat tolerance, and work clothing or protective gear raises heat stress; none of these are modeled. OSHA notes outdoor workers have died of heat stroke on days when the maximum heat index was only 86 °F. [7]
